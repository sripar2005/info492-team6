Checking every handoff against the contract

An AI agent that reads each step from RFI answer to change order, checks it against the contract's rules when it happens, and routes anything wrong to a person. People still make every decision.

Thesis

T-101

Cost and schedule problems on construction projects often start less with the work itself and more with handoffs: information moving from one document to the next without anyone checking it against the contract's rules. The handoffs we study run from an RFI answer, to a change notice, to a priced proposal, to a change order.

Our claim

An AI agent that checks each handoff can catch at least 80% of rule violations on a change proposal when it is submitted, within a few days, instead of in a post-payment audit months or years later.

The gap

9.7days median to answer an RFI
22%of RFIs never formally answered
22days on average for a sub to price a change
26more days to get a signed change order
77%of subs have written off change work

Why

The biggest losses seem to happen between steps, not inside them. After an RFI is answered, subcontractors spend an average of 22 days pricing a change and wait another 26 for a signed change order. GCs most often cut payment because of disputed pricing (66%) or thin backup (53%). Errors and omissions in contract documents have also been the top cause of major disputes in most recent years.

Under the standard AIA A201 general conditions, an RFI answer does not approve added cost on its own. It has to go through the contract's change process, and owners often change the response times and notice windows from project to project. It is easy for a busy project engineer to miss some of those rules.

How

Each handoff has rules that can be written down: whether an answer is a clarification or a change, when notice is due, what the fee already covers, and which charges are excluded. We pull those rules out of the contract, check each document against them as it moves to the next step, and flag problems right away. The Webster School audit points in this direction. Its auditor found questionable change pricing only after final payment, and recommended reviewing change proposals for these errors when they are submitted.

Our four predictions are on D-402; what the agent may and may not do is on D-404.

The RFI figures come from a 2013 Navigant Construction Forum study; the change order figures come from a vendor-commissioned 2026 Dodge/Clearstory survey. We treat them as rough estimates.

How the checks work

T-102
RFI answer
Change notice
Priced proposal
Change order
Check 1 · Answer → notice

Does the RFI answer just clarify, or does it change the work? If it changes it, when must notice go out?

Check 2 · Notice → proposal

Does the priced proposal stay within the contract's scope, fee, and excluded charges?

Check 3 · Proposal → change order

Does the change order match the priced proposal and the contract's fee and pricing rules?

Example

A proposal applies a 5% fee to negotiated support services, which the Webster audit later found was wrong. The agent flags it at submission, cites the clause, and routes it to the project manager.

Synthetic data plan

L-202

Webster's full RFI and change logs are not public, so we generate proposals priced under the real contract terms and plant known mistakes in them.

  1. Use the real rules

    Every made-up proposal is priced under the actual Webster contract terms quoted in the audit.

  2. Plant known mistakes

    We insert the audit's error types, late notices, and scope-changing answers, and record the correct label for each.

  3. Keep the volume realistic

    Past studies average 9.9 RFIs per $1M of construction, about 290 for Webster. We use fewer, with the same share left unanswered.

  4. Write everything down

    We share the generator, random seeds, label definitions, and the share of planted cases.

  5. Report what we actually did

    Results are about catching planted issues. If a public records request gets us real logs, we re-run on them.

Coordination posture: the GC's seat

P-301

Subcontractors cannot contract directly with the design team or owner, so every question and price passes through the general contractor twice: questions and prices go up, answers and money come down. Our agent works from the GC's seat.

Owner

Seattle Public Schools

Approves and pays; the board votes on larger changes.

Design team

TCF Architecture

Answers RFIs and issues supplemental instructions.

GC/CM · our seat

BNBuilders

Passes questions up, prices changes, combines sub prices. Also self-performed several scopes, about $9.85M billed in the final payment application.

Subcontractors

Trade partners

Find field conflicts, price changes, and do the work.

Post-hoc reviewer

District audit firm

Reviewed billing after final payment. In our design, its checks move earlier.

Why the GC seat matters at Webster

The audit found the GC/CM's share of self-performed work grew from 29.8% at the bid package stage to about 40% by the end, against a 30% limit in state law, and those scopes got no competing bids. That puts the GC in two roles at once, coordinator and a company doing the work, which raises the question of who checks what.

Guardrails and limits

D-404
The agent can
  • Read
  • Extract
  • Classify
  • Cite the clause
  • Route to a person
Needs a named approver
  • Answer an RFI for the design team
  • Tell a sub to proceed
  • Commit the GC to a price or position
  • Send a notice
  • Close a record

We will test that each of these needs a recorded approval.

Limits

One project and made-up proposals cannot prove this works everywhere. They can show whether the idea is workable and where it breaks. If rules are hard to pull from real contract language, that is still a useful result.
