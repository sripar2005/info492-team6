13 peer reviews. You reported the highest headline number in the room — roughly 94.9% recall on core rules — and you made the right design choice in requiring exact quotes from the contract rather than paraphrase. Reviewers picked up on that: a rule you can trace to verbatim contract language is auditable, and your audit-fee example made the use case concrete for people who'd never read a construction contract. The pairing of a high number with a verifiable output format is a strong combination.
The room was less convinced than your number suggests, and it's worth understanding why. Only 6 of 13 reviewers said your claim was clearly falsifiable — the softest read of any presenting team — and two said it couldn't lose. The reason is almost certainly your answer key: it was written by the person who wrote the contract. When the same party authors both the document and the ground truth for what the document says, 94.9% measures agreement with one person's reading, not correctness. That is a real result, but it's a narrower one than it sounds, and reviewers sensed the gap without always being able to name it. One reviewer also noted role boundaries weren't explicitly tested — your agent extracts rules, but nothing established that it stops short of interpreting what a rule means for a given dispute.
For Demo #2, make the answer key independent. Have someone who did not write the contract mark up the same rules, then report how often your two keys disagree before you report the agent's score. If the human-to-human disagreement rate is 10%, a 94.9% agent score means something very different than if it's 1%. That one addition would turn your strongest number into your most defensible one. And add the inverse test: feed it a contract section that contains no extractable rule and confirm it returns nothing rather than inventing one.

4 peer reviews, and the most-praised single design decision in the class. Four reviewers independently named the same thing as their favorite part: the AI picks the deadline, and deterministic code calculates the date. "AI picks DDL and code calculates the date — it's very smart," one wrote; "I like code can do the date math," said another. That split is the correct architecture, and the reason is worth saying out loud in your next demo: date arithmetic has exactly one right answer, so handing it to a language model adds risk for nothing. Knowing which parts of a problem should never touch the model is a more advanced instinct than most teams in the room have. Your evaluation setup drew the same respect — two separate 30-case sets, answer keys, an 85% target stated in advance, and a discussed holiday edge case.
Now the hard part. You reported 60 out of 60 correct, exceeding your 85% target, and two of your most careful reviewers flagged it rather than celebrating it. One wrote that "the 100% accuracy results might be a little iffy" while crediting you for addressing the issues; the other named the cause: "testing covered only one contract and short synthetic documents." They are right, and this is the same independence problem your 94.9% raised after Demo #1. A perfect score on a self-authored key over one contract measures agreement with your own reading of that contract. It is a real result about a narrow thing, and it reads as a broad claim.
The credibility move for Demo #3 is counterintuitive: go find the score that isn't 100%. Add a second contract you didn't write the key for, have someone outside the team label a subset, and report your human-to-human disagreement rate before you report the agent's. If two careful humans disagree on 8% of rules, your agent's number means something very different than if they disagree on none. You'd also benefit from the inverse test — feed it a section containing no extractable rule and confirm it returns nothing rather than inventing one. Two reviewers found the explanations long-winded and dense; bullet points would help.
